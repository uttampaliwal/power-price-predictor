# Why Conformal Prediction Matters for Electricity Price Forecasting

**TL;DR:** Point forecasts alone are dangerous in volatile electricity markets. We applied conformal prediction to Indian day-ahead electricity prices and found it delivers near-perfect coverage guarantees — but systematically fails during price spikes. Here's what that means for risk management.

---

## The Problem: Point Forecasts Are Not Enough

If you're a trader at an Indian distribution company (DISCOM), and you get a forecast of ₹3,000/MWh for tomorrow's evening peak, you need to know: **how confident should I be in this number?**

A point forecast of ₹3,000/MWh with a 90% prediction interval of [₹2,500, ₹3,500] is fundamentally different from one with [₹1,000, ₹5,000]. The first tells you the price is likely near ₹3,000. The second tells you the model has no idea.

Yet most electricity price forecasting papers — and most production systems — report only point metrics: RMSE, MAE, R². They tell you the average error, but not the uncertainty around individual predictions. For trading, hedging, and risk management, this is like driving a car with only a speedometer and no fuel gauge.

## Enter Conformal Prediction

Conformal prediction (CP) is a distribution-free framework that wraps any point predictor to produce prediction intervals with **guaranteed coverage**. The key guarantee:

> Regardless of the data distribution, the probability that the true value falls within the predicted interval is at least 1 - α.

For electricity prices, this is powerful because:
1. **No Gaussianity assumption** — prices are heavy-tailed, ceiling-pinned, and regime-dependent
2. **Works with any model** — wrap XGBoost, LightGBM, ridge, or a neural network
3. **Finite-sample guarantee** — the guarantee holds for any sample size, not just asymptotically

The basic idea is simple:
1. Split your data into training and calibration sets
2. Train your model on the training set
3. Compute residuals (|y - ŷ|) on the calibration set
4. Use the (1-α) quantile of those residuals as your prediction interval margin

That's Split Conformal Prediction (SCP). It's shockingly simple and yet provides a mathematically rigorous guarantee that parametric intervals (Gaussian, t-distribution) cannot match.

## What We Did

We applied three conformal prediction methods to Indian day-ahead electricity prices at 15-minute resolution:

| Method | How It Works | Tradeoff |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| **Split Conformal (SCP)** | Fixed margin from calibration residuals | Simple, exact coverage, constant width |
| **Adaptive Conformal (ACP)** | Rolling window of recent residuals | Adapts to regime shift, wider intervals |
| **Conformalized QR (CQR)** | Quantile models + conformal calibration | Adaptive width, but under-covers |

We tested on **233,000 block-level observations** from the Indian Energy Exchange (IEX), spanning January 2020 to August 2026, with a strict 20-month untouched holdout (January 2025 to August 2026).

The base learners were XGBoost, LightGBM, and ridge regression — all trained on lagged prices, calendar features, and rolling statistics.

## The Results

### Near-Perfect Marginal Coverage

The headline finding: **Split Conformal with LightGBM achieves 91.4% coverage** (target: 90%). That's an absolute gap of only +1.4 percentage points — essentially exact calibration.

| Base Model | CP Method | PICP | Gap from 90% |
|-----------|-----------|------|-------------|
| XGBoost | SCP | 89.97% | **-0.03%** |
| LightGBM | SCP | 91.39% | +1.39% |
| Ridge | SCP | 88.97% | -1.03% |
| XGBoost | ACP | 93.25% | +3.25% |
| LightGBM | ACP | 93.60% | +3.60% |

The SCP method delivers on its theoretical promise: distribution-free, assumption-free coverage that works on volatile electricity prices.

### But Spikes Break the Guarantee

Here's the critical finding: **all conformal methods under-cover during price spikes.**

During normal periods (below 85th percentile of prices), coverage is 90-95% — on target or slightly over. During price spikes (above 85th percentile), coverage drops to **79-86%** — a deficit of 7-11 percentage points.

| Regime | SCP (XGBoost) | ACP (XGBoost) |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Normal | 92.0% | 94.9% |
| Spike | **79.4%** | **84.8%** |

Why does this happen? Price spikes are generated by different data-generating processes than normal operation — heat waves, coal supply shortages, demand surges. These extreme events violate the exchangeability assumption that underlies conformal prediction. The calibration scores from normal periods don't generalize to spike conditions.

### The Practical Implication

For a trader using conformal intervals to size positions:
- During normal operation: the intervals are reliable
- During spikes: **you're under-hedged exactly when it matters most**

This is not a failure of conformal prediction — it's a fundamental limitation of exchangeability-based methods during distributional shift. The solution is to combine conformal methods with regime-aware risk limits.

## CQR: Narrow but Unreliable

Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) produces the narrowest intervals (PINAW = 57.9 vs 94.0 for SCP), but under-covers at 84.7%. This is because the quantile regression models are trained on a subset of the data, and their estimation error on the heavy-tailed price distribution isn't fully compensated by conformal calibration.

**Takeaway:** Narrow intervals aren't always better. If they come at the cost of violated coverage guarantees, they create false confidence.

## What This Means for Power Markets

### For Risk Managers
1. Use SCP + LightGBM for standard risk management tasks where average coverage is sufficient
2. Use ACP during high-volatility regimes (monsoon, summer peak) when spike coverage is critical
3. Always supplement conformal intervals with regime-aware risk limits

### For Researchers
1. The spike-regime under-coverage is an open problem — regime-aware conformal methods are needed
2. Online conformal methods that continuously update calibration scores could improve adaptive performance
3. CP is model-agnostic — it could wrap any point predictor, including deep learning architectures

## How to Use It

```python
from probabilistic import SplitConformal, AdaptiveConformal
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

# Train your base model
model = Ridge(alpha=1.0)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

# Split conformal
scp = SplitConformal(alpha=0.10)
scp.fit(model, X_cal, y_cal)
intervals = scp.predict(model, X_test)
# intervals has columns: y_pred, lower, upper

# Adaptive conformal
acp = AdaptiveConformal(alpha=0.10, window=500)
acp.fit(model, X_cal, y_cal)
intervals = acp.predict(model, X_test)

# Evaluate coverage
from probabilistic import evaluate_conformal
metrics = evaluate_conformal(y_test, intervals, alpha=0.10)
# metrics: PICP, PINAW, Winkler, ACI, regime-stratified coverage
```
